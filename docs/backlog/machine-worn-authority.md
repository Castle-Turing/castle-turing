# What does a machine-authored "no" do to the resident's name?

*A research question, not deferred implementation. Citations from
memory; **[verify]** marks the unconfirmed.*

**What.** `docs/vision.md` states it with complete clarity and then
never picks it up again: "When the agent declines a meeting, someone on
the other end is being told no by a machine wearing the user's
authority; the failure modes there are social, not technical."

Every other design question in this project is about the resident. This
one is about the third party, and it is the only place where a mistake
lands on someone who never consented to the system existing.

**The question.** What does third-party knowledge — or later discovery —
of machine authorship do to the social force of an outward action taken
in the resident's name? And does non-disclosure, once discovered, cost
the principal more standing than the delegation saved?

Wrongly answerable in both directions: disclosure could be harmless
honesty, or could collapse the interaction's effectiveness, or
concealment-then-discovery could be the uniquely damaging path.

**Where the answer likely lives.** AI-mediated communication: Hancock et
al.'s AI-MC framing (*JCMC* 2020); Hohenstein & Jung and successors on
smart replies shifting blame, and on discovered AI assistance reducing
interpersonal trust **[verify]**. Chatbot disclosure: Luo et al.
(*Marketing Science* 2019 **[verify]**) — from memory, disclosing
machine identity up front sharply reduced effectiveness despite
identical content, which is the most uncomfortable possible result for a
project committed to legibility. Computers-as-social-actors: Reeves &
Nass, *The Media Equation* (1996). Politeness theory: Brown & Levinson
(1987) — a refusal is a canonical face-threatening act, which is
precisely why human assistants exist partly *as* face-threat buffers.
Elish's "moral crumple zones" (2019) for where blame lands when the
agent errs.

**What would change.** One line in the authority taxonomy, but the line
with third parties on the other side of it: whether refusals — the most
face-threatening outward acts — always require either disclosure
("Castle, for the resident") or resident review before sending, and
whether drafted-in-the-resident's-voice is reserved for low-stakes acts.

**Priority: lower, but with a deadline.** Outward autonomous action is
the latest-built tier — early slices keep drafts in a folder and the
resident on send. But the taxonomy document should be written by someone
who has read this, so it needs to land before that document freezes its
outward-facing row. See [[authority-taxonomy-prior-art]].
