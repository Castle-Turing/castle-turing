## 1. The requirements-engineering inspection literature

**The foundational Fagan data is not what the field cites it as.** I could not obtain Fagan's 1976 *IBM Systems Journal* paper from any authoritative host — the ACM DL entry returns 403 and IEEE returns 418 **[V, negative]**. I did retrieve Fagan's own 2002 retrospective chapter ("Reviews and inspections", in *Software Pioneers*), and it contains no defect-detection percentage at all; its only quantitative claim is that inspections consume 20–30% of the effort during the first half of product development, offered while rebutting the objection that inspections delay shipment **[V]** (https://eden.dei.uc.pt/~mvieira/Fagan02.pdf). The chapter does confirm that the method was applied to requirements specifications, design, code, test plans and user documentation, so its scope genuinely includes prose artifacts **[V]**. The widely repeated "inspections find 60–90% of defects" figures trace to secondary restatements rather than to a primary table I could verify; Kemerer and Paulk report the range as 30% to over 90% across techniques with trained inspection teams starting around 60%, citing Fagan, Radice and others rather than measuring it themselves **[V]** (https://sites.pitt.edu/~ckemerer/PSP_Data.pdf). Treat every Fagan percentage you encounter as **[R]** until someone shows you the table.

**The strongest single experiment on requirements-document reading is Porter, Votta and Basili 1995, and its headline number is smaller than the folklore.** Forty-eight graduate students formed sixteen three-person teams, each inspecting two software requirements specifications using ad hoc, checklist, or scenario-based detection **[V]**. Team fault detection rates averaged 0.43 (ad hoc), 0.41 (checklist) and 0.57 (scenario) on the water-level monitoring specification, and 0.31, 0.24 and 0.45 on the cruise-control specification; the authors summarize this as "an improvement of roughly 35%" for the scenario method **[V]** (https://api.drum.lib.umd.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/332d6328-bdce-4726-9102-e00bac748f9a/content). Two secondary results matter more than the headline. First, "the Checklist method — the industry standard, was no more effective than the Ad Hoc detection method" **[V]**. Second, and this is the calibration point that should anchor any expectation of what document review achieves: even the best method left a three-person team detecting only 45–57% of the known faults **[V]**.

**Perspective-based reading, the technique most often invoked to justify assigning distinct reviewer roles, does not survive aggregation.** The original NASA/GSFC study by Basili, Green, Laitenberger, Lanubile, Shull, Sørumgård and Zelkowitz used 12 to 14 professional subjects per run and constructed "teams" post hoc by taking the union of individual reviewers' defect lists **[V]** (https://www.cs.umd.edu/~mvz/handouts/emp_pbr.pdf). In the 1994 pilot, the reading-technique main effect was non-significant in both domains (p = 0.2148 generic, p = 0.9629 NASA) **[V]**. In the 1995 run the generic-domain individual effect reached p = 0.0019, with PBR reviewers detecting 32.14% versus 24.64% for their usual technique, which the authors describe as a 30% improvement **[V]**. At the simulated-team level, the pilot reached only p = 0.0660 on generic documents, while the 1995 run was significant at 0.05 in both domains **[V]**. The authors themselves flag that individual-level improvement was "an unexpected result, since the true benefit of PBR is expected to be seen at the level of teams" **[V]**.

**The replication record is where the technique falls apart, and the pattern in it is an allegiance effect.** Wohlin's FOSE 2013 evidence-profile chapter enumerates the twelve sources and twenty-two studies that Ciolkowski's 2009 quantitative aggregation collected, and evaluates each one directly **[V]** (https://www.wohlin.eu/fose13.pdf). Three studies were excluded for lacking any comparison technique; twelve produced no significant result at all; four significant results favoured PBR with industrial participants, of which three are the original Basili study; one significant student study favoured PBR; and two studies produced significant results *in favour of checklist-based reading over PBR* **[V]**. Wohlin's verdict is that "the evidence is quite inconclusive", followed by the observation that decides the matter: "all studies with significant results include one or more of the authors of the original paper" **[V]**. The secondhand summary of Ciolkowski's own conclusion — no significant difference between PBR and ad hoc on requirements documents, and PBR less effective than checklists — is consistent with this enumeration but I could not fetch Ciolkowski's abstract directly **[R]** (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/ESEM.2009.5316026 returns 403; the IEEE Xplore page returned empty).

**One replication isolates the actual mechanism, and it is not the perspectives.** Maldonado, Carver, Shull, Fabbri, Dória, Martimiano, Mendonça and Basili (*Empirical Software Engineering* 11, 2006) found that in the original study the three perspectives found largely non-overlapping defect sets, whereas in their replication two of the three perspectives found similar sets on one document — and "interestingly this document was the only case where the users of PBR were not more effective than the users of a checklist" **[V]** (http://carver.cs.ua.edu/Papers/Journal/2006/EMSE_11_1.pdf). The operative variable is whether the assigned roles actually produce non-overlapping search, not whether roles were assigned. Sandahl, Blomkvist, Karlsson, Krysander, Lindvall and Ohlsson published an extended replication in the same journal in 1998; I verified its metadata but the abstract is elided by the publisher **[V metadata, R conclusion]** (https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1009724120285).

**The defect taxonomy the field settled on is small, orthogonal, and directly portable to briefs and handovers.** Porter, Votta and Basili used a composite of schemes from Schneider et al. and Basili and Weiss, splitting faults into *omission* (important information left unstated) and *commission* (incorrect, redundant or ambiguous information added), with omission subdivided into Missing Functionality, Missing Performance, Missing Environment and Missing Interface, and commission into Ambiguous Information, Inconsistent Information, Incorrect or Extra Functionality, and Wrong Section **[V]**. Their design nests the three techniques: the checklist is a refinement of the taxonomy and the scenarios are procedures implementing specific checklist items, so scenario responsibilities are strict subsets of checklist responsibilities, which are strict subsets of ad hoc responsibilities **[V]**. Moving from ad hoc to checklist to scenario buys detail at the cost of coverage.

## 2. Requirements smells and automated ambiguity detection

**The best-measured smell detector achieves 59% precision.** Femmer, Méndez Fernández, Wagner and Eder derived Requirements Smells directly from ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 and implemented them in a prototype called Smella, evaluated across three industrial cases and one university case: "The automatic detection yields an average precision of 59% at an average recall of 82% with high variation" **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.08847, published as *JSS* 123:190–213, doi:10.1016/j.jss.2016.02.047). Per-smell recall ran between 0.84 and 0.95 for four of five investigated smells, with the Comparative smell highest at 0.95, while the Superlative smell reached only 0.5 on few data points **[V]**. Crucially the authors define a smell as an indicator rather than a defect: "Whether a Requirements Smell finding is or is not a problem in a certain context must be individually decided for that context and is subject to reviews" **[V]**. In one case, practitioners judged 65% of the discussed findings relevant, with the irrelevant remainder explained by domain knowledge the outsider lacked, by deliberate process requirements, and by findings located in the non-binding rationale portion of a user story **[V]**.

**The independent industrial dataset puts naive lexical smell detection at roughly 30% precision by construction.** QuRE (Femmer, Houdek, Unterbusch, Vogelsang) releases 2,111 industrial requirements annotated through a real review process used under contract for close to a decade; 633 are labelled defect and 1,478 labelled acceptable **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08868v1). Because every row contains at least one weak word from a 23-word catalogue, the labels mean that only about 30% of weak-word occurrences are genuine defects, with the other 70% being contextually appropriate uses of the same word **[V]**. The same paper documents the field's data problem bluntly: 44 of 57 requirements-quality datasets are inaccessible, publicly available ones have a median of 20 requirements, and only 7 were annotated by practitioners despite 42 originating from them; for QuRE itself the authors "could not retrieve any information on the consistency of the experts' labeling, such as inter-rater agreement metrics" **[V]**.

**The QuARS lineage's own false-positive claim is a vendor claim and is contradicted by the measured data.** The QuARS authors report that "the presence of false positives has been observed in every case study … The rate of false positives with respect to actual defects has been rarely over 10%" **[V]** (https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2376/NLP4RE19_paper07.pdf). No measurement protocol, sample, or annotation procedure accompanies that figure, and it is irreconcilable with Femmer's 59% precision and QuRE's 30% defect rate on the same class of lexical indicator. Treat it as an author claim. NASA's ARM tool (Wilson, Rosenberg and Hyatt, ICSE 1997) is a measurement instrument rather than a detector — it counts occurrences of quality indicators such as weak phrases and produces a statistical report on word frequencies at structural levels of the document, and I found no precision or recall validation associated with it **[R]** (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/253228.253258; the Semantic Scholar page returned empty on fetch).

**ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148's nine characteristics split cleanly into the lintable and the unlintable.** The standard defines *appropriate, complete, conforming, correct, feasible, necessary, singular, unambiguous, verifiable* **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.10886, Table I). What has been operationalized is the surface layer: dictionary lookups for subjective language, ambiguous adverbs and adjectives, loopholes, and open-ended non-verifiable terms; morphological analysis or POS tagging for superlatives, comparatives and negative statements **[V]** (Femmer et al., Table 3). Template-conformance checking — whether a requirement matches a pattern such as EARS — reaches precision above 0.85 and recall above 0.9 **[V, secondhand within a verified source]** (Femmer et al., reporting on RETA). What has resisted automation is everything that requires knowing what the system is for: completeness, correctness, necessity, and feasibility have no operational detector, because a checker cannot know what is missing.

**Rule-based and LLM detectors have now been compared head-to-head and neither is satisfactory.** Pereira, Costa and Parreira Júnior evaluated RETA, Tactile Check, Tiger Pro and ChatGPT on requirements documents containing over 85 anomalies, and concluded that "all analyzed tools, including ChatGPT, demonstrated unsatisfactory levels of recall and precision", with Tactile Check the best performer and ChatGPT offering advantages in visualization and interaction flexibility but performance "not satisfactory compared to tools specifically designed for anomaly detection" **[V]** (https://doi.org/10.5753/jserd.2026.5919). Unterbusch and Vogelsang report the most favourable LLM numbers I found, on the narrow task of judging whether a weak word is genuinely defective in context: zero-shot GPT-4.1-mini with chain-of-thought reached precision 0.573 at recall 0.997 (F1 0.728), rising to precision 0.679 and recall 0.972 (F1 0.799) after twenty human-validated examples, versus a BERT model fine-tuned on 320 examples reaching precision 0.635 and recall 0.804 **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01952). The shape of that result is the important part: the model flags nearly everything (recall 0.97–1.00) and human validation buys precision, with gains flattening after about twenty examples.

**The whole enterprise rests on rules whose downstream harm has almost never been measured.** Frattini, Montgomery, Fischbach, Méndez, Fucci and Unterkalmsteiner surveyed the 57 primary studies on requirements quality factors and found that 17 of 57 (29.8%) report no impact on any downstream activity whatsoever; where impact is reported at all, the evidence is dominantly hypothesized (19/40, 47.5%) rather than inductive (11/40, 27.5%) or referenced (10/40, 25%); cost is reported in 9 of 57 studies and affected resources in 5 of 57, and in every case "only hypothesized or referenced, never determined empirically" **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.10355). Their summary judgment is that "requirements quality research focuses on normative rules and mostly fails to connect requirements quality to its impact on subsequent software development activities" **[V]**. A requirements smell is a style rule with a plausible story attached, not a measured predictor of downstream failure.

**Nocuous ambiguity is the one construct that took the "does it actually matter" question seriously.** Chantree, Willis, Kilgarriff, de Roeck and later Yang, de Roeck, Gervasi, Willis and Nuseibeh distinguish ambiguity that different readers actually resolve differently (nocuous) from ambiguity everyone resolves the same way (innocuous), establishing ground truth by collecting multiple human interpretations rather than by rule; the best reported model reached 76.25% accuracy on 200 expert-labelled anaphoric instances **[R]** (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1858996.1859007, https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/110487/ — I did not obtain the accuracy figure first-hand).

## 3. LLM-era artifact review, 2024–2026

**The one controlled experiment on LLM-assisted document inspection found the assistance made humans worse.** Broccia, Frattini, Arora, ter Beek, Fantechi, Vogelsang and Ferrari ran a crossover experiment with 34 participants inspecting textual specifications with and without LLM support, analysed with a Bayesian regression model per outcome variable: "Results show that LLM support negatively affects smell detection accuracy but has no significant effect on smell classification or task duration. A learning effect is present across experimental periods, but reduced when RI is first performed with LLM support" **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21298, 21 August 2026). Their conclusion is that LLM support "does not necessarily improve performance and may, instead, hinder it for novice inspectors", and that learning inspection with LLM support from the beginning "may slow down the skill acquisition process" **[V]**. A secondary write-up quantifies the gap as roughly 8% higher F₁ unassisted with overlapping 95% credibility intervals, and a learning-rate split of roughly 12% versus 7% improvement between periods for the unassisted-first and assisted-first cohorts **[R]** (https://www.beri.net/article/llm-requirements-inspection-f1-drop-novice-inspector-skill-acquisition). This is the single most directly relevant finding in the report and it points the wrong way for anyone hoping a model in the loop makes human review of briefs better.

**The field has barely looked.** The systematic review of LLMs for requirements engineering by Zadenoori, Dąbrowski, Alhoshan, Zhao and Ferrari covers 74 primary studies from 2023–2024 and finds only 4 of them (5%) address requirements defect detection, against 15 each on elicitation and validation — a substantial shift away from the defect-detection focus of pre-LLM NLP4RE **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/html/2509.11446v1). Seventy-five percent of evaluations are laboratory experiments and only 16% use public datasets, creating what the authors call a replicability problem; the review does not address hallucination or false-positive rates at all and does not quantify them **[V]**.

**Where an LLM's document judgments have been checked against human ground truth, agreement collapses on the realistic artifact.** Ronanki and colleagues at Graz instructed an LLM (Llama-2-70b-chat) to assess requirements against all nine ISO 29148 characteristics, with at least four software engineers per requirement establishing ground truth by majority vote. The Cohen's kappa between LLM and participants was 0.4028 on a ten-requirement toy Stopwatch project and 0.0486 on the larger DigitalHome project under independent assessment, rising to 0.7545 and 0.2223 respectively under a "bound" assessment condition **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.10886). The paper's own abstract and conclusions describe the LLM as accurately identifying "the majority of requirements with quality flaws" **[V]**; the measured kappa of 0.05 on the more realistic project does not support that framing, and the discrepancy is itself a data point about how LLM-review results get reported.

**The CriticGPT seeded-bug design has been run on prose, and deterministic checks beat the model reviewer by better than two to one.** Nam, Jeong and Kim injected 27 identical defects into manuscript fixtures and compared a suite of 21 deterministic detectors against a single-prompt LLM reviewer: the deterministic gates detected all 27 with zero false positives on clean fixtures, while the LLM reviewer detected 11 of 27 and missed errors in code, bibliography and style **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09500). Their design principle is worth stating as they state it — resolve each integrity question "with the cheapest sufficient mechanism", using deterministic checks where feasible and prose-level analysis only when necessary **[V]**. The caveat is structural: the deterministic gates were built by the same authors against the same defect catalogue, so 27/27 measures coverage of an enumerated list rather than generalization, and the comparison is one prompt against a purpose-built suite.

**Cross-section defects — exactly the class that a stale handover exhibits — are detected worst, and orchestration destroys the ability to find them.** Fukui tested ten systems across five model generations and five providers on contradictions spanning distant document sections, and found that "every model that finds these cross-section defects under a single agent loses that ability under orchestration, detection falling two-thirds or more"; six of the tested models discriminated above chance at all, and the collapse was not mitigated by scale or extended reasoning **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26174). Model diversity did not prevent it: the failure appeared in every paradigm tested **[V]**. If your review pipeline chunks a long state document across sub-agents, this is the finding that says the chunking itself removes the capability you wanted.

**On documents specifically, model panels buy far less independence than their headcount suggests.** Kohli tested a panel of nine frontier LLMs from seven model families across three natural-language inference datasets and found the nine judges "effectively provide only about 2 independent votes' worth of information", with roughly 75% of nominal independence lost, panel accuracy falling 8 to 22 percentage points below what independent voting would predict, and established aggregation methods closing at most 11% of that gap; the best individual model matched or exceeded the full panel in every condition tested **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29800). Kim, Garg, Peng and Garg evaluated over 350 LLMs and found that on one leaderboard dataset "models agree 60% of the time when both models err", that shared architectures and providers drive correlation, and — the finding that specifically undercuts cross-vendor panels — that "larger and more accurate models have highly correlated errors, even with distinct architectures and providers" **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962). Cross-vendor diversity is a real but shrinking hedge, and it shrinks precisely as the models you would actually deploy get better.

**The one place LLM document review has a defensible track record is as a redundant second opinion on long-form prose.** Liang et al. compared GPT-4-generated feedback against human peer review across 3,096 Nature-family papers and 1,709 ICLR papers, finding overlap of 30.85% and 39.23% respectively with human reviewers, against human-human overlap of 28.58% and 35.25% — the model agreed with a human reviewer about as often as two human reviewers agreed with each other **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01783). In a prospective study with 308 researchers from 110 institutions, 57.4% found the feedback helpful or very helpful and 82.4% found it more beneficial than feedback from at least some human reviewers **[V]**. Overlap was reportedly larger on weaker (rejected) papers, around 43.80% **[R]**. The correct reading is not that the model reviews well; it is that human document review has low inter-reviewer agreement in the first place, which is the same conclusion Porter's 24–57% team detection rates deliver from the other direction.

## 4. Verifying derived artifacts against their sources

**Automated traceability never established that its numbers meant anything.** Borg, Runeson and Ardö classified 79 publications on information-retrieval-based trace recovery and concluded that "most previous studies do not go beyond reporting precision and recall of candidate trace links from evaluations using datasets containing less than 500 artifacts", identifying a need for industrial case studies and noting that only one small industrial pilot existed **[V]** (http://mrksbrg.com/emse15_borg/, *EMSE* 19(6):1565–1616). Notably, their attempt to aggregate and compare accuracy across studies was rejected by reviewers on methodological grounds — comparing disparate datasets was not defensible — so the field has no meta-analytic accuracy figure, only a scatter of incomparable per-dataset numbers **[V]**. The "precision/recall ceiling" story is therefore better told as a *commensurability* failure than a ceiling.

**Transformer-era trace recovery reports large relative gains and no absolute accuracy.** Lin, Liu, Zeng, Jiang and Cleland-Huang's T-BERT achieved "average improvements of 60.31% measured using Mean Average Precision" over a vector-space-model baseline on issue-to-commit links in three open-source projects, with Single-BERT most accurate and Siamese-BERT comparable at far lower runtime **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.04411). The abstract gives no absolute MAP or F1, which is the same reporting habit Borg et al. criticized.

**The closest measured analogue to "does this document still describe the thing it describes" is code-comment inconsistency detection, and its just-in-time framing is the right one.** Panthaplackel, Li, Gligoric and Mooney frame the task as detecting "whether a comment becomes inconsistent as a result of changes to the corresponding body of code, in order to catch potential inconsistencies just-in-time, i.e., before they are committed to a code base" **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01625, AAAI 2021). Their abstract reports only that the approach "outperforms multiple baselines by significant margins" and gives no F1, accuracy or dataset size **[V, negative]** — the numbers exist in the paper's Table 2 but I did not obtain them.

**Naive LLM prompting for document-versus-artifact divergence produces a false-positive rate that makes it unusable.** Xu, Wahab, Holmes and Lemieux state the problem directly: "Plain use of LLMs for this task yield unacceptably high false positive rates: LLMs identify natural gaps between high-level documentation and detailed code implementations as inconsistencies" **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00215v1). The measured baseline is stark — the plain LLM approach flagged 98% of functions as inconsistent with 14% accuracy; their Local Categorization, External Filtering method reduced the flag rate from 98% to 14% and raised accuracy from 14% to 94% **[V]**. Even after filtering, function-level precision, recall and F1 on Python were 0.64 each with accuracy 0.92, and inconsistency-level precision across four languages ranged from 0.52 (Java) to 0.70 (TypeScript) on a 1,615-function benchmark **[V]**. The false-positive taxonomy is instructive: missing API knowledge accounted for 8–89% of false positives across datasets, incorrect reasoning 11–61%, and "under-promises" — implementation detail the document simply does not mention — up to 18% **[V]**. A model reading a state document against a repository will, by default, report every abstraction as a contradiction.

**Multi-agent decomposition helps recall on document-versus-artifact checking when the aggregation is designed for it.** Liu, Zhao, Yuan, Xu, Xu, Zhang and Ngai's Dude system for paper-code discrepancy detection reports a single-LM baseline at 58.70% recall, 91.53% precision and 71.52% F1, versus 80.43% recall, 93.67% precision and 86.55% F1 for the dual-detection multi-agent system, and on 20 recent papers identified 45 valid discrepancies against 3 invalid ones **[V]** (https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03416). They also note that a vanilla multi-agent configuration found the most valid discrepancies while producing the highest number of invalid ones, giving significantly low precision **[V]**. This sits in direct tension with Fukui's orchestration cliff, and the reconciliation appears to be that decomposition helps on *localized* discrepancies while destroying detection of *cross-section* ones.

## 5. Inspection economics and reviewer redundancy

**Review rate is the one process variable with clean, large-sample, non-allegiance evidence behind it.** Kemerer and Paulk analysed two datasets of 371 and 246 programs from Personal Software Process data using regression and mixed models, controlling for developer ability, and found review rate a significant factor in defect removal effectiveness in all four model fits **[V]** (https://sites.pitt.edu/~ckemerer/PSP_Data.pdf, *IEEE TSE* 35, 2009). Reviewing at 200 LOC/hour or less identified nearly two-thirds of defects in design reviews and more than half in code reviews **[V]**; effectiveness declines above that rate, with the authors quantifying the improvement from staying within the limit at 8–15% for design reviews and 10–11% for code reviews, and a 25–33% difference at the higher rate comparison **[V]**. The finding that most changes how you should spend effort is the flat top: "once the 200 LOC/hour limit has been met, a more deliberate pace seems to not materially improve performance" **[V]**. Within the same paper, Buck's optimal inspection rate of 125 LOC/hour with rapid decline above it, Weller's finding that preparation rate and product familiarity were the two dominant factors, and Gilb and Graham's 0.5–1.5 pages per hour for documents are all cited as prior work **[V for the citation, R for each original]**.

**Collection meetings contribute nothing on average, and this is measured, not asserted.** Porter, Votta and Basili measured meeting gain rates of 4.7 ± 1.3% and 3.1 ± 1.1% across their two specifications, and net meeting improvement of −0.9 ± 2.2% and −1.2 ± 1.7%, concluding that "on the average, collection meetings contributed nothing to fault detection effectiveness" **[V]**; they cite Votta's independently measured gain rate of 3.9 ± 0.7% as telling the same story **[V]**. Kemerer and Paulk cite Eick et al. finding 90% of defects identifiable in preparation alone **[V for the citation, R for the original]**. Whatever value a synchronous review discussion has, defect discovery is not it.

**Nobody has shown that adding reviewers to a document raises detection, and the null results are underpowered.** Porter, Siy, Mockus and Votta varied team size and the number and sequencing of inspection sessions on a live Lucent 5ESS compiler project and found that these structural changes did not significantly influence defect detection rate, while certain combinations dramatically increased inspection interval; their conclusion was that "the 'inputs' into the inspection process (reviewers, authors, and code units) were significant sources of variation" and that improving detection *techniques* beats restructuring the *process* **[V]** (https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/5e99e954-e625-46c1-9a07-d8ec9ac5b107). Boodoo, El Emam, Laitenberger and Madhavji then showed why such null results should not be trusted: a Monte Carlo power analysis calibrated to their own UML design inspection data found that an experiment comparing 2-person against 4-person teams with five teams per group has only 58% power against Cohen's recommended 80%, and that reaching adequate power requires 60 to 90 professional subjects — "quite unlikely in practice" **[V]** (https://www.ehealthinformation.ca/web/default/files/wp-files/2000-The-Optimal-Team-Size-UML.pdf, NRC ERB-1081). Their own economic analysis concluded there is no single optimal team size — it depends on the relative cost of finding the defect later and on whether and how long a meeting is held — and that "contemporary suggestions of only two-person inspection teams are far from optimal" **[V]**.

**The most useful single number for calibrating an automated reviewer's output came out of an industrial inspection study.** Boodoo et al. report that in Porter et al. 1997, "approximately 80% of the issues identified during preparation were not true defects" **[V for the report of it, R for the original]**. A four-in-five noise rate is what trained professional reviewers produced on real code under a formal process. It is the baseline any prose-review automation should be measured against, and it explains why the meeting-loss literature is not obviously a story about losing good findings.

**The economics of document inspection versus code inspection have not been separated in the literature I could reach.** The recommended rates differ nominally (100–200 lines of text per hour for design documents versus 100–200 LOC per hour for code, per the rule set Kemerer and Paulk summarize **[V]**), but I found no study that measures whether the defect-yield-per-hour curve differs in shape between prose and code artifacts.

## Dry angles (searched, found nothing — reported as findings)

1. **No study seeds defects into agent handoff, state, or task-brief documents and measures detection.** The nearest analogues are the clinical-manuscript integrity gates (27 seeded defects), DocPrism (code-documentation), Dude (paper-code), and Fukui's cross-section defects. The specific artifact class this report is about has no measurement literature.
2. **No measured evidence that cross-vendor LLM panels help on prose defect detection.** The panel evidence I verified is on natural language inference judging (Kohli) and leaderboard/resume tasks (Kim et al.), not on finding defects in documents. Fukui's ten systems across five providers is the closest, and it found the failure mode was universal across providers.
3. **No measured work on documentation-staleness detection as such**, outside the code-comment inconsistency line. There is no equivalent of "this state document was true three commits ago" with a measured detector.
4. **Fagan's 1976 primary source is not reachable** from ACM (403) or IEEE (418), and his own 2002 retrospective contains no defect-detection percentage. Every "inspections find N% of defects" number in circulation should be treated as unverified until someone produces the table.
5. **Ciolkowski's 2009 aggregation abstract is not fetchable** from ACM DL or IEEE Xplore; its conclusion is carried here only by Wohlin's independent, verified re-evaluation of the same twelve sources.
6. **The Rosadini/Ferrari railway-domain precision figures are paywalled**; Springer redirects to an identity provider and Semantic Scholar has the abstract elided. The 1,866-requirement study and its "discard patterns" for false-positive reduction remain **[R]**.
7. **Springer, IEEE Xplore, ACM DL and ScienceDirect systematically block direct fetching.** Everything verified here came through arXiv, author homepages, institutional repositories (DRUM, CEUR, CNR, NRC), or the Semantic Scholar API. Any research protocol that assumes publisher access will produce mostly **[R]** claims.

## Judgment calls

1. **I did not re-summarize the two companion reviews and I positioned against them where they touch.** The correlated-errors material here is deliberately restricted to panel-composition evidence (effective vote count, cross-provider correlation) rather than restating the LLM-judge findings already established.
2. **I treated Wohlin's FOSE 2013 chapter as primary for the PBR replication record.** It is a secondary synthesis, but it re-downloaded and independently evaluated all twelve sources rather than restating Ciolkowski, and it is the only account I could fetch that enumerates each study's outcome. The alternative was carrying the whole PBR verdict as **[R]**.
3. **I owe a correction on PBR.** My first-pass framing, based on the original NASA study and the standard textbook account, was that perspective-based reading is an established improvement over ad hoc reading. The aggregation evidence reverses that: twelve of twenty-two studies non-significant, two significant *against* PBR, and every significant pro-PBR result co-authored by someone from the original team. I have not seen the allegiance pattern in this literature flagged as prominently as it deserves.
4. **I flagged the ISO 29148 LLM study's kappa against its own abstract.** Ronanki et al. conclude the LLM "accurately identifies the majority of requirements with quality flaws" while reporting κ = 0.0486 on their larger project. I reported both rather than picking one, because the gap between reported conclusion and reported statistic is itself relevant to how LLM-review claims should be read.
5. **I discounted the 27/27 deterministic-gate result.** Detectors built by the authors against the authors' own enumerated defect catalogue measure catalogue coverage, not generalization; the 11/27 LLM figure is also one prompt, not a tuned reviewer. The *ordering* is credible, the ratio is not a general effect size.
6. **The JSERD tool-comparison table came back garbled from the fetcher.** I carried only the qualitative conclusion (all tools unsatisfactory, Tactile Check best) and deliberately did not report the per-tool precision and recall figures, because I could not confirm which number attached to which tool.
7. **I let the Fukui and Dude results stand in tension rather than reconciling them.** One says decomposition destroys cross-section defect detection; the other says decomposition raises recall by 22 points. My reading is that they measure different defect locality, but that reconciliation is my inference and not stated in either paper.
8. **I extracted several PDFs with a hand-written zlib/FlateDecode text extractor** after the fetcher failed on them. Numbers quoted from those extractions (Basili PBR, Porter/Votta/Basili, Wohlin, Frattini, Femmer, Kemerer/Paulk, Boodoo, Fagan 2002) were read from decompressed content streams with whitespace stripped; I re-read the surrounding context for each number, but transcription risk is nonzero and higher than for HTML-sourced figures.

## Design implications

1. **Set the expectation that a single review pass over a brief finds roughly half its defects, not most of them**, because the best-performing method in the only well-powered requirements-inspection experiment left three-person teams at 45–57% detection (Porter/Votta/Basili).
2. **Do not assign distinct "perspectives" to reviewers as a structural rule, and if you do, assign them by non-overlapping search coverage rather than by role name**, because PBR's advantage vanished in twelve of twenty-two studies and disappeared specifically in the one document where two perspectives converged on the same defects (Wohlin; Maldonado et al.).
3. **Adopt the omission/commission taxonomy as the review checklist for briefs and handovers**, since it is the one artifact of this literature that replicated as a description of what goes wrong in prose specifications: missing functionality, performance, environment and interface; ambiguous, inconsistent, incorrect and misplaced information (Porter/Votta/Basili).
4. **Budget review by reading rate, not by artifact count, and stop optimizing past the threshold**, because defect removal effectiveness is significantly rate-dependent up to roughly 200 lines per hour and materially flat below it (Kemerer & Paulk, 371 and 246 programs).
5. **Impose a hard length cap on any single brief or state document rather than adding reviewers to a long one**, since structural changes to team size and session count showed no significant effect on detection while reviewer, author and artifact identity dominated the variance (Porter/Siy/Mockus/Votta), and the null results on team size are underpowered anyway (Boodoo et al.).
6. **Prefer one deterministic checker over one more model reviewer for anything mechanically decidable** — cross-references resolve, named files exist, numbers match between sections, required header keys present — because deterministic gates recovered 27 of 27 seeded document defects against a single-prompt LLM reviewer's 11 of 27 (Nam et al.), and because a lexical rule alone runs at about 30–59% precision on requirements text (QuRE; Femmer et al.).
7. **Never let a model report a spec-versus-artifact divergence without a filtering stage, and measure the filter**, because unfiltered LLM prompting flagged 98% of functions as inconsistent at 14% accuracy, with abstraction gaps and missing API knowledge as the dominant false-positive causes — exactly the shape a state document takes relative to a repository (DocPrism).
8. **Do not chunk a long handover across sub-agents for review**, because every model tested that could detect contradictions spanning distant document sections lost that ability under orchestration, with detection falling by two-thirds or more, and no provider was exempt (Fukui).
9. **Treat the second vendor in a cross-vendor panel as a modest hedge with a known decay, not as an independent reviewer**, because nine judges across seven families supplied about two effective votes, panels fell 8–22 points below independent-voting predictions, and error correlation is *higher* among the more accurate models even across providers (Kohli; Kim et al.).
10. **Keep a human reading the brief unaided before the model sees it, and record findings before showing the model's**, because the only controlled experiment on LLM-assisted inspection found assistance reduced detection accuracy and slowed skill acquisition when it came first (Broccia et al.) — and because a machine-generated finding list is subject to the same four-in-five noise rate that human preparation already produces (Porter et al. 1997, via Boodoo et al.), which is a dispositions problem before it is a detection problem.
